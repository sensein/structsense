SUMMARY 44 
We generated a multi-region, subcellular-resolution spatial transcriptomic atlas of the 45 
human basal ganglia by integrating MERFISH+ and Stereo-seq across four neurotypical 46 
donors.  These datasets profiled ~7 million cells spanning the caudate, putamen, 47 
nucleus accumbens, and globus pallidus, resolving 60 transcriptionally distinct cell types.  48 
We show region-selective, molecular and spatial diversification of medium-spiny-neuron 49 
cell types and multiple non-neuronal populations with distinct molecular identities and 50 
spatial localizations.  Subcellular RNA localization captures somatic size and projection-51 
inferred signatures that reflect direct and indirect pathway topology.  Cellular community 52 
analyses reveal the enrichment of sub-clusters of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes at 53 
striosome–matrix borders, while primate-expanded interneurons are confined to matrix 54 
territories.  Cross-species mapping uncovers orthologous striosome–matrix organization 55 
and conserved dorsolateral-ventromedial gene expression gradients. This atlas 56 
provides a foundational molecular and spatial framework for studying human basal 57 
ganglia architecture, offering a multi-centimeter scale resource that links cell types, 58 
spatial architecture, and subcellular transcript topography across multiple nuclei. 59

INTRODUCTION  61 
The human basal ganglia is a critical brain structure involved in motor control, learning 62 
and emotion.  It is comprised of distinct anatomical nuclei / subdivisions including the 63 
striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen), the globus pallidus (external and internal 64 
segments, GPe and GPi), and the nucleus accumbens (NAc) 1.  Basal ganglia cell types 65 
and architecture are well characterized in rodents and non-human primates, but are 66 
much less understood in humans.  The literature from both animal models and humans 67 
shows that GABAergic medium spiny neurons (MSNs) are the dominant neuronal cell 68 
type in the striatum (85–95%) 2-5; MSNs are divided into two major populations: D1-type 69 
MSNs, which express the dopamine receptor D1 (DRD1), tachykinin precursor 1 (TAC1), 70 
and dynorphin; and D2-type MSNs, which express the dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2), 71 
proenkephalin (PENK), and enkephalin 2,3,6.  The D1 and D2 populations are spatially 72 
organized into interdigitated striosome and matrix compartments that form the structural 73 
foundation of basal ganglia function 3,7,8.  Further studies, including those using single-74 
nucleus RNA-seq in animal and human brains have revealed additional D1/D2 neuronal 75 
diversity 9,10, and identified multiple classes of interneurons, including cholinergic 76 
(choline acetyltransferase-positive; ChAT+), parvalbumin-positive (PV+), somatostatin-77 
positive (SST+), and neuropeptide Y/tyrosine hydroxylase-positive (NPY+/TH+) 78 
subtypes 10-14. The nucleus accumbens, located at the ventral extension of the striatum, 79 
contains both D1 and D2 MSNs and is further divided into core and shell subregions. 80 
These subregions mirror the cellular composition of the dorsal striatum, including 81 
diverse interneuron classes 10,15. The globus pallidus consists of two principal divisions: 82 
the external segment (GPe) and the internal segment (GPi) 3,16.  Both regions are 83

A comprehensive understanding of human basal ganglia organization requires 89 
integrating cell-type identity, molecular signatures, and spatial architecture within an 90 
intact anatomical framework.  Traditional single-cell RNA sequencing provides 91 
molecular resolution but lacks spatial context, limiting its ability to capture circuit-level 92 
organization.  Emerging spatial transcriptomic technologies map gene expression 93 
directly within intact tissue sections that retain the spatial relationships of cell types and 94 
structures 10,14,22-24.  These methods include probe-based multiplexed fluorescent in situ 95 
hybridization (FISH) and next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based spatially resolved 96 
transcriptomics, each with complementary strengths in resolution, transcriptome 97 
coverage, and throughput 23,25,26.  These approaches enable an unprecedented 98 
mapping of brain architecture through quantitative classification of cell types and 99 
detection of spatially patterned gene expression.  Although previous studies have 100 
revealed the spatial organization of striosomes within the striatal matrix and 101 
transcriptional gradients across basal ganglia nuclei in model organisms such as mice 102 
and macaques 10,27, a comprehensive molecular and cell-type-resolved spatial atlas of 103 
the adult human basal ganglia remains lacking.  This gap has persisted in part because 104 
of the large size and complex cytoarchitecture of human basal ganglia nuclei, as well as 105 
technical limitations in the spatial scale and resolution of existing mapping approaches.  106

Addressing this limitation is particularly critical given the central role of the basal ganglia 107 
in human movement disorders (including Parkinson’s disease and Huntington’s disease), 108 
neuropsychiatric conditions, and species-specific differences in basal ganglia cellular 109 
composition and structural organization that reflect the complexity of inputs and outputs 110 
for these structures in humans 7,28. 111 
Recent advances in large-scale spatially resolved transcriptomics have transformed our 112 
ability to characterize cellular heterogeneity and molecular architecture within intact 113 
neural tissue such as human basal ganglia.  Two particularly powerful approaches are 114 
Multiplexed Error-Robust Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (MERFISH) 26,29-33 and 115 
Spatio-Temporal Enhanced Resolution Omics-sequencing (Stereo-seq) 34-38.  MERFISH 116 
enables highly multiplexed, single-molecule RNA imaging at subcellular resolution, 117 
allowing the precise mapping of hundreds to thousands of genes within individual cells 118 
while maintaining their spatial context 26,29,31.  This approach has been instrumental in 119 
decoding cell-type organization across cortical and subcortical structures in multiple 120 
species that reveal both conserved and species-specific architectural principles in the 121 
mouse, monkey and human brains 14,27,36,39-41.  Subsequent advancements in MERFISH 122 
have expanded high-resolution spatial transcriptomics into a multimodal platform 123 
capable of imaging DNA, RNA, and proteins within the same tissue specimen 42,43. Most 124 
recently, the incorporation of modified probe designs, high-throughput microscopy, and 125 
microfluidic automation has led to MERFISH+ 44, an enhanced implementation that 126 
enables imaging across multi-centimeter–scale tissue areas. This extension achieves 127 
large-area spatial transcriptomics with subcellular precision for integrative molecular 128 
and structural analyses 44.   Stereo-seq employs DNA nanoball-patterned arrays to 129

Molecular and spatial taxonomy of cell types in the human basal ganglia 180 
Using MERFISH+, we resolve approximately 60 transcriptionally distinct cell types that 181 
are separable when embedded in Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection 182 
(UMAP) space of gene-expression data (Figure 2A).  These cell types show expression 183 
of expected corresponding marker genes (Figure S2) and demonstrate strong 184

are eight neuronal classes that include caudal and lateral ganglionic eminence 189

neurons (MSNs) (Figure S3A).  191

DISCUSSION 452 
By integrating the two high-throughput spatial transcriptomics platforms (MERFISH+ 453 
and Stereo-seq), we generated a multiscale spatial transcriptomic atlas of the human 454 
basal ganglia spanning 6 orders of magnitude in scale from subcellular transcript 455 
organization (~50 nm) within single-cells to the multi-centimeter (~4cm) spatial 456 
arrangement of cell types across basal ganglia nuclei. This large-scale atlas, which 457 
covers ~7 million cells in total, encompasses all major nuclei including the caudate, 458 
putamen, nucleus accumbens, and the globus pallidus across four neurotypical 459 
postmortem brains.  In addition to providing a comprehensive molecular and anatomical 460 
reference for the neuroscience community, our work establishes a scalable 461 
experimental and computational framework for performing large-area, high-resolution 462 
spatial transcriptomics in human tissue. 463 
While much of the spatial organization of cell types and circuit domains in the human 464 
basal ganglia has been derived from rodent and non-human primate studies 13,67, our 465 
dataset provides the first comprehensive spatial and molecular reference directly in 466 
humans. The resulting framework reveals how subcellular gene expression, cell type 467

MERFISH library was designed using a similar pipeline described in previous studies 42-1303

complementary to the mRNA sequence of a gene, three 20nt readout sequences unique 1305 
for each gene and two 20nt PCR primers. However, we made one modification to this 1306 
design by adding a 4th readout sequence in order to help us to troubleshoot MERFISH 1307

overlapping 40-mer target sequences from the mRNA sequence of each gene. Next, we 1309

each sequence to 4 readout and 2 primer sequences. Finally, we screened the 1314 
concatenated sequences for homology against the whole genome and excluded primary 1315 
probes that had homology scores beyond 99 percentiles. 1316

Stereo-seq data processing  1415 
We processed the Stereo-seq data using the standard analysis workflow software 1416 
(https://github.com/STOmics/SAW, RRID:SCR_025001) version 8.3 with the following 1417 
parameters:  –kit-version “Stereo-seq T FF V1.2”, – sequencing-type “PE100_50+100”. 1418 
The pipeline takes three files as an input: (1) paired-end FASTQ files, (2) mask file and 1419 
(3) mosaic image of nuclei-staining. The first read in the fastq files contains coordinate 1420 
ID (CID) - spatial location identifier barcode sequence that is unique to each DNA nano 1421 
ball on the stereo-seq chip, and the Molecular ID (MID) - artificially synthesized mRNA-1422 
specific sequence that helps differentiate the number of reads contributed by mRNA 1423 
expression level due to amplification. The CIDs were first aligned and matched with the 1424

MERFISH image processing 1438 
MERFISH image analysis consists of 4 major steps: (1) detecting individual 1439 
fluorescence spots; (2) correcting the drift between the images across the different 1440 
hybridization rounds; (3) decoding the gene identity of fluorescence spots by matching 1441 
their on-off intensity pattern with the binary words in the codebook; (4) segmenting the 1442 
mask of individual cells. A detailed overview of each step is as follows.  1443 
1. Spot detection: we applied flat-field correction to the raw transcript images in the 1444 
Cy3, Cy5 and 750 channels to minimize vignetting artifacts. Next, we deconvolved the 1445 
images by applying the Wiener’s algorithm as implemented in the sdeconv package 1446 
using a point spread function (PSF) estimated from the raw transcript images. Then, we 1447 
normalized the resulting images and detected spots with gaussian peaks.  1448 
2. Drift correction: To estimate the drift between hybridization rounds, we detected 1449 
image features corresponding to local minima and local maxima intensity points within 1450 
the DAPI channel using the same procedure as in the spot detection step. These 1451 
features were then used to register the images from different hybridization rounds using 1452 
phase cross-correlation.  1453
